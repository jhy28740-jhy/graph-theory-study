Abstract.

We prove a sharp quadratic inequality between the largest two eigenvalues $\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}$ of a graph with $n$ vertices.
We also prove a quadratic inequality between the second and third largest eigenvalues $\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}$ .
These results in particular imply the bounds $\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\leq\frac{8}{7}n-2$ , $\lambda_{3}\leq\frac{n}{3}-1$ and $\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}\leq\frac{2}{3}n-2$ .
In fact we determine the closure of the set of possible $(\frac{\lambda_{1}+1}{n},\frac{\lambda_{2}+1}{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ and $(\frac{\lambda_{2}+1}{n},\frac{\lambda_{3}+1}{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ .
More generally, we prove quadratic bounds in the case of symmetric matrices in $[0,1]^{n\times n}$ , and we also give a quadratic bound for two eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix in $[-1,1]^{n\times n}$ .
These bounds are proved by transforming the problem into extremal geometric questions in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ .
We use the method of Lagrange multipliers to reduce to special cases with at most five points, and we deal with these special cases directly.

Key words and phrases: graph eigenvalues, matrix eigenvalues

1. Introduction

In this article we study the first, second and third largest eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix $A_{G}$ of a graph $G$ , and more generally, of a symmetric matrix $A\in[0,1]^{n\times n}$ .
Let $\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dotsc\geq\lambda_{n}$ be the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix $A_{G}$ , where $G$ is a finite, simple graph with $n\geq 2$ vertices, and let $s_{i}=\frac{\lambda_{i}+1}{n}$ .
It is known that $s_{1}\leq 1$ and $0\leq s_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}$ (see [3]), and recently $s_{3}\leq\frac{1}{3}$ has been proved by Tang [8] (also see [7]), solving a problem of Nikiforov [6].
Recently Kumar, Liu, Monterde, Pragada and Tait [5] proved $\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\leq\frac{8}{7}n$ , solving a conjecture of Ebrahimi B, Mohar, Nikiforov and Ahmady [1], and Huang and Wei [4] improved this to $s_{1}+s_{2}\leq\frac{8}{7}$ .

We study the possible values of $(s_{1},s_{2})\in\R^{2}$ and $(s_{2},s_{3})\in\R^{2}$ .
We prove that $s_{1}^{2}+s_{1}s_{2}+2s_{2}^{2}\leq s_{1}+s_{2}$ and $2(s_{2}^{2}+s_{2}s_{3}+s_{3}^{2})\leq s_{2}+s_{3}$ , and we prove that these bounds are sharp in a sense (Corollary 2.1).
As a consequence we get $s_{1}+s_{2}\leq\frac{8}{7}$ , $s_{2}+s_{3}\leq\frac{2}{3}$ and $s_{3}\leq\frac{1}{3}$ .
We prove quadratic inequalities not just for graphs, but more generally, for symmetric matrices $A\in[0,1]^{n\times n}$ (Theorem 2.1).
We also prove a quadratic bound for two eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix $B\in[-1,1]^{n\times n}$ (Theorem 2.2).
We prove these results as a consequence of extremal geometric statements in $\R^{3}$ and in $\R^{2}$ (Propositions 2.1, 2.2, 2.3 and 2.4).
In the last extremal geometric problem (in the proof of Proposition 2.4), we use the method of Lagrange multipliers, and reduce the problem to the case of at most $5$ points (with further restrictions as well), and we deal with these special cases directly.

We use the notation $[n]=\{1,\dotsc,n\}$ for $n\in\Z_{\geq 0}$ .
For symmetric square matrices $A\succeq B$ means $A-B$ is positive semidefinite, and $A\succ B$ means $A-B$ is positive definite.
If $G$ is a finite simple graph with vertex set $[n]$ , then $A_{G}$ denotes its adjacency matrix.
The characteristic polynomial of a matrix $M\in\R^{n\times n}$ is $\chi_{M}(t)=\det(tI_{n}-M)$ .
Let $\mcalR_{1,2}$ denote the set of $(s_{1},s_{2})\in\R^{2}$ such that there is a sequence $(G_{i})_{i\geq 1}$ of finite, simple graphs, where $G_{i}$ has $n_{i}$ vertices and eigenvalues $\lambda_{i,1}\geq\dotsc\geq\lambda_{i,n_{i}}$ , and $n_{i}\to\infty$ and $\frac{\lambda_{i,a}+1}{n_{i}}\to s_{a}$ as $i\to\infty$ for $a\in\{1,2\}$ .
Similarly, let $\mcalR_{2,3}$ denote the set of $(s_{2},s_{3})\in\R^{2}$ such that there is a sequence $(G_{i})_{i\geq 1}$ of finite, simple graphs, where $G_{i}$ has $n_{i}$ vertices and eigenvalues $\lambda_{i,1}\geq\dotsc\geq\lambda_{i,n_{i}}$ , and $n_{i}\to\infty$ and $\frac{\lambda_{i,a}+1}{n_{i}}\to s_{a}$ as $i\to\infty$ for $a\in\{2,3\}$ .
Let $\mcalS_{1,2}$ and $\mcalS_{2,3}$ be slight modifications of $\mcalR_{1,2}$ and $\mcalR_{2,3}$ , where we do not require $n_{i}\to\infty$ in the definition.
So $\mcalS_{1,2}$ is the closure of the set $\{(\frac{\lambda_{1}(G)+1}{|V(G)|},\frac{\lambda_{2}(G)+1}{|V(G)|});\,|V(G)|\geq 2\}\subseteq\R^{2}$ and $\mcalS_{2,3}$ is the closure of the set $\{(\frac{\lambda_{2}(G)+1}{|V(G)|},\frac{\lambda_{3}(G)+1}{|V(G)|});\,|V(G)|\geq 3\}\subseteq\R^{2}$ .
Clearly $\mcalR_{1,2}\subseteq\mcalS_{1,2}$ and $\mcalR_{2,3}\subseteq\mcalS_{2,3}$ .
In Corollary 2.1 we give exact descriptions for $\mcalR_{1,2}$ , $\mcalR_{2,3}$ , $\mcalS_{1,2}$ , $\mcalS_{2,3}$ .

2. Statement of the results

The following theorem gives a quadratic inequality between the first and second largest eigenvalues, and an inequality between the second and third largest eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix in $[0,1]^{n\times n}$ .

Theorem 2.1.

Let $n\geq 2$ , and let $A\in[0,1]^{n\times n}$ be a symmetric matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dotsc\geq\lambda_{n}$ .
Let $s_{i}=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{n}$ .
Then $s_{1}\in[0,1]$ and $s_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}$ .
If $s_{2}\geq 0$ , then

(1)

 $s_{1}^{2}+s_{1}s_{2}+2s_{2}^{2}\leq s_{1}+s_{2}.$ 

If $n\geq 3$ and $s_{3}\geq 0$ , then

(2)

 $2(s_{2}^{2}+s_{2}s_{3}+s_{3}^{2})\leq s_{2}+s_{3}.$

The construction in the proof of Corollary 2.1 shows that the bounds here are sharp in some sense (assuming $s_{2}\geq 0$ in the first inequality, and $s_{3}\geq 0$ in the second one).

 $s_{1}^{2}+s_{1}s_{2}+2s_{2}^{2}\leq s_{1}+s_{2},$

We deduce the quadratic inequality for $s_{1},s_{2}$ in Theorem 2.1 from the following $2$ -dimensional geometric statement.

Proposition 2.1.

Let $p_{1},\dotsc,p_{n}\in\R_{\geq 0}$ , $z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n}\in\R^{2}$ such that $\sum_{i}p_{i}=1$ and $\sum_{i}p_{i}z_{i}z_{i}^{T}\preceq I_{2}$ .
Let $A\in[0,1]^{n\times n}$ be symmetric, and let

 $\Delta=\sum_{i,j}A_{i,j}p_{i}p_{j}z_{i}z_{j}^{T}\in\R^{2\times 2}.$ 

Let $s_{1}\geq s_{2}$ be the eigenvalues of $\Delta$ .
Then $s_{1}\leq 1$ and $s_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}$ , and if $s_{2}\geq 0$ , then

 $s_{1}^{2}+s_{1}s_{2}+2s_{2}^{2}\leq s_{1}+s_{2}.$ 

Similarly, we deduce the quadratic inequality for $s_{2},s_{3}$ in Theorem 2.1 from the following $3$ -dimensional geometric statement.

Proposition 2.2.

Let $p_{1},\dotsc,p_{n}\in\R_{\geq 0}$ , $z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n}\in\R^{3}$ such that $\sum_{i}p_{i}=1$ and $\sum_{i}p_{i}z_{i}z_{i}^{T}\preceq I_{3}$ .
Let $A\in[0,1]^{n\times n}$ be symmetric, and let

 $\Delta=\sum_{i,j}A_{i,j}p_{i}p_{j}z_{i}z_{j}^{T}\in\R^{3\times 3}.$ 

Let $s_{1}\geq s_{2}\geq s_{3}$ be the eigenvalues of $\Delta$ .
Then $s_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}$ , and if $s_{3}\geq 0$ , then

 $2(s_{2}^{2}+s_{2}s_{3}+s_{3}^{2})\leq s_{2}+s_{3}.$ 

We will prove Proposition 2.1 using Proposition 2.2.
In the proof of Proposition 2.2, we use an orthogonal projection from $\R^{3}$ onto a plane orthogonal to $\sum_{i}p_{i}z_{i}$ .

Theorem 2.2.

Let $B\in[-1,1]^{n\times n}$ be a symmetric matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_{1}$ , …, $\lambda_{n}$ .
Let $\alpha=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{n}$ , $\beta=\frac{\lambda_{j}}{n}$ for some $i\neq j$ .
Then $\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}\leq 1$ , and if $\alpha\beta\geq 0$ , then

(3)

 $\alpha^{2}+\alpha\beta+\beta^{2}\leq|\alpha+\beta|.$ 

Remark 2.2.

We give two constructions to show that the bound here is sharp in some sense.
First, let $n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}\geq 1$ , $n=n^{\prime}+n^{\prime\prime}$ , $B\in\{-1,1\}^{n\times n}$ , where $B_{i,j}=-1$ if and only if $\max(i,j)\leq n^{\prime}$ .
Then

 $\chi_{B}(t)=t^{n-2}(t^{2}-(n^{\prime\prime}-n^{\prime})t-2n^{\prime}n^{\prime\prime})=t^{n-2}(t-n\alpha)(t-n\beta)$ 

with

 $\alpha=\frac{1}{2n}(n^{\prime\prime}-n^{\prime}+\sqrt{(n^{\prime}+n^{\prime\prime})^{2}+4n^{\prime}n^{\prime\prime}}),\ \beta=\frac{1}{2n}(n^{\prime\prime}-n^{\prime}-\sqrt{(n^{\prime}+n^{\prime\prime})^{2}+4n^{\prime}n^{\prime\prime}}).$ 

Then $\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=1$ .
Here $\alpha>0>\beta$ only depend on $\frac{n^{\prime}}{n^{\prime\prime}}$ , and $(\alpha,\beta)\to(1,0)$ as $\frac{n^{\prime}}{n^{\prime\prime}}\to 0$ , and $(\alpha,\beta)\to(0,-1)$ as $\frac{n^{\prime}}{n^{\prime\prime}}\to\infty$ , so we can approximate all points of the circle arc between these (see figure 3).
Taking $-B$ we get the opposite circle arc too.

For our second construction, let $n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\geq 1$ , $n=n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}$ , $B\in\{-1,1\}^{n\times n}$ symmetric, where for $i\leq j$ let $B_{i,j}=B_{j,i}=-1$ if and only if $i\leq n_{1}$ and $j>n_{1}+n_{2}$ .
Then

 $\chi_{B}(t)=t^{n-3}(t^{3}-nt^{2}+4n_{1}n_{2}n_{3})=t^{n-3}(t-n\alpha)(t-n\beta)(t-n\gamma)$ 

for some $\alpha\geq\beta\geq\gamma$ .
Here $(t-\alpha)(t-\beta)(t-\gamma)=t^{3}-t^{2}+c$ with $c=4\frac{n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}}{n^{3}}\in(0,\frac{4}{27}]$ (with $c=\frac{4}{27}$ only for $n_{1}=n_{2}=n_{3}$ ), and $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\to(1,0,0)$ as $c\to 0$ , and $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(\frac{2}{3},\frac{2}{3},-\frac{1}{3})$ at $c=\frac{4}{27}$ .
So $\alpha\geq\beta>0>\gamma$ (because the zeros depend continuously on $c$ , see e.g., [2]), $\gamma=1-\alpha-\beta$ , and $0=\alpha\beta+\alpha\gamma+\beta\gamma=\alpha\beta+(\alpha+\beta)(1-\alpha-\beta)=\alpha+\beta-\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}-\alpha\beta$ .
We could of course take $(\beta,\alpha)$ instead of $(\alpha,\beta)$ , so we can approximate the whole ellipse arc between $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ (see figure 3).
Taking $-B$ we also get the opposite ellipse arc.
